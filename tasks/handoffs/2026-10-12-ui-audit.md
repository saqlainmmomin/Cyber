### A5. Additions to Saqlain's prompt

1. **Stack or oversight.** Saqlain wants to know which problems come from the stack and which were overlooked. Classify every root cause as exactly one of:
   - **Stack**: caused by Jinja, HTMX or the lack of a component model.
   - **Design decision**: a Yozora token or principle produced it.
   - **Shortcut**: the system allowed the right thing and the screen did something else (inline styles, hand-written markup).
   - **Never designed**: no one specified it.

2. **Measure, do not eyeball.**

   - Type: for every screen, the count of text nodes at each rendered font size. Flag any screen where audit content (questions, conclusions, evidence quotes, criteria) renders below 14px.
3. **Rule on Yozora.** The prompt below says to avoid glass and gradients. Today's design is glass on a gradient canvas. For each of P1 to P10 in `docs/product/yozora-design-system.md`, and for the canvas, the glass surfaces, the 8 accent presets and the status colours, give a ruling: keep, change (say how) or retire, with the evidence. Put it in `ROOT_CAUSES.md` under its own heading. This ruling is a recommendation for Saqlain to accept or reject at the stop point.

## Part B. Saqlain's audit prompt

You are performing a full UI/UX audit of this product before/during its migration from HTMX to Next.js + Tailwind.

Do NOT start redesigning individual screens immediately.

First understand the product, inventory the interface, inspect the actual running application, identify systemic design problems, trace those problems back to the code/component architecture, and produce a concrete migration design specification.

### PRODUCT CONTEXT

This is a cybersecurity / compliance assessment product used by auditors and consultants.

Typical workflows include:

- creating and managing assessments
- defining assessment scope
- requesting evidence from clients
- reviewing uploaded evidence
- completing detailed questionnaires
- evaluating controls
- reviewing AI-generated or pre-filled conclusions
- identifying missing evidence
- creating findings
- generating reports
- navigating large ISO 27001-style control sets
- reading substantial quantities of explanatory text and evidence

This means the interface is fundamentally a KNOWLEDGE-WORK interface.

The primary user may spend hours reading, comparing, reviewing, approving and editing information.

The UI must therefore optimize for:

1. readability
2. scanability
3. information hierarchy
4. low cognitive fatigue
5. efficient navigation
6. high information density without visual clutter
7. clear status differentiation
8. progressive disclosure
9. predictable interaction patterns
10. professional enterprise-product quality

### CURRENT PROBLEM

The current application works functionally, but the visual system feels unfinished and tiring.

Known examples:

- Questionnaire content is squeezed into a narrow column while large areas of the viewport are unused.
- Long-form text is difficult to read.
- Text contrast is too low in several places.
- Important information does not visually separate itself from secondary information.
- Headers, descriptions, metadata, statuses and actions often have similar visual weight.
- Large blocks of information appear as undifferentiated walls of text.
- Repeated evidence rows/cards do not provide enough scanning cues.
- Many screens feel like database output rather than a deliberately designed product.
- Dense information is presented without enough structure.
- Some screens have excessive empty space while the actual work area is cramped.
- Some screens use cards where tables or structured lists may work better.
- Some screens use large containers for very little information.
- Statuses such as critical, needs attention, partially implemented, pending, approved, missing evidence etc. are not sufficiently distinct.
- Navigation hierarchy is unclear in places.
- Tabs, sub-tabs, breadcrumbs and sidebar navigation compete with one another.
- Reading width is inconsistent.
- Visual rhythm is weak.
- Actions are sometimes detached from the information they affect.
- Important audit decisions do not always feel important.
- Repetitive information causes visual fatigue.
- Some layouts appear centred/narrow merely because that was easy to implement rather than because it supports the task.

Do not limit yourself to these issues. They are examples of the classes of problems that exist.

Produce a comprehensive UI/UX audit of the ENTIRE application and a design specification for the Next.js migration.

The result should answer:

"What is wrong with the current UI, why does it feel wrong, what systemic causes created these problems, and what should the Next.js version do instead?"
