How to map controls
The core skill for this job. Spend the most time here.
  The big ideaPick one master list of controls. Map every regulation and framework to that master list, once. Don't map the frameworks to each other. With 5 sources, that's 5 mappings instead of 10 side-by-side comparisons, and it stays manageable as sources are added.
  The chain you're building
    SourceA law, framework or internal policy
    →
    RequirementOne single "must", with its reference
    →
    ControlWhat the client actually does
    →
    TestHow you prove the control works
  Every control also records its owner, the risk it reduces, and where it applies (which entity, which country, which systems). Links go many-to-many, so keep them in a separate mapping table rather than comma-separated in one cell.
  The 10 steps
    List your sourcesWrite down every regulation, framework and internal policy in scope, with its version, country and which entities it covers.
    Break them into single requirementsOne "must" per row. Keep the exact wording and the reference (e.g. DORA-Art19-1). Split sentences that ask for several things.
    Pick the master structure (the "anchor")Domains → control objectives → controls. Reuse a catalogue (NIST 800-53, ISO 27002, SCF) or build your own. My pick: the client's own domain names, ISO 27002 / 800-53 level of detail, and CSF 2.0 for reporting.
    Map each requirement to controlsSay how well they match (equal, smaller, bigger, overlapping, none) and why. Then mark coverage as full, partial or none.
    Clean up the listMerge duplicates, split controls that do two jobs, and remove controls that serve no rule and no risk. Where sources disagree on a number (like patch time), use the strictest and note where it came from.
    Write the controls properlyUse the formula below. One control = one goal, one owner, one test.
    Link everything upYou should be able to answer both ways: "Which controls cover DORA Article 9?" and "If I delete this control, which rule loses coverage?" This is called traceability.
    Compare with today → gap registerCheck the client's current controls against the new library. Label each gap: missing, badly designed, not working, or not documented.
    Plan the fixes → roadmapGroup gaps into projects. Order by deadline, risk, dependencies and effort. Quick wins first. Basics (asset list, access control, governance) early.
    Keep it alive → governanceName an owner, set review dates, and agree what triggers an update (new law, audit finding, incident).
  Worked example: the 10 steps for ISO 27001 + NIST CSF + CIS
  The scenarioBuild one control library that lets a client stay ISO 27001 certified, report to the board on NIST CSF 2.0, and meet CIS v8.1 IG2. To keep it concrete, most examples follow one thread: access control. The IDs, names and numbers below are illustrative, not from a real client.

    What you do: make one table of everything in scope, and decide early how each source will be used.

    What you do: split every clause, subcategory and safeguard until each row holds one ask. Give each row an ID.
      OriginalBecomes
      NIST CSF PR.AA-05: "Access permissions, entitlements, and authorizations are defined in a policy, managed, enforced, and reviewed, and incorporate the principles of least privilege and separation of duties"6 rows: PR.AA-05-a defined in policy · -b managed · -c enforced · -d reviewed · -e least privilege · -f separation of duties

    Rule of thumb: CIS safeguards are already close to single asks. CSF subcategories often hide 3–6 asks each. ISO clauses 4–10 become governance and ISMS-process requirements.

  4Map each requirement to controls
    What you do: for every requirement row, name the control(s), the match type, the coverage and a one-line reason.
    Example: what maps to IAM-03 Quarterly access review?
      RequirementMatchCoverageWhy
      CSF PR.AA-05-d "reviewed"EqualFullSame ask
      ISO A.5.18-b review access rightsEqualFullSame ask, once split in step 2

  5Clean up the list
    What you do: merge, split and remove controls, and set one value for every number the sources disagree on.
      MergeVulnerability management shows up as ISO A.8.8, CSF ID.RA-01 and PR.PS-02, and CIS 7.1–7.7. Built source by source, that's 10+ controls. After merging: VUL-01 process, VUL-02 scanning, VUL-03 fixing.
      SplitThe client's old control "Access is managed and reviewed" does three jobs. It becomes IAM-01 grant, IAM-02 remove, IAM-03 review.
      Set the numbersCIS gives hard frequencies (internal scans at least quarterly, external monthly, fixes monthly). ISO and CSF just say "timely". So CIS sets the floor and the client's risk appetite sets anything stricter.
      RemoveAn old "annual firewall rule sign-off" control with no requirement or risk behind it, already covered by NET-02's change review. Retired, with the reason logged.
  6Write the controls properly
    What you do: write each control with the formula (who · what · when · how · why · proof), then fill in its attributes.
    IAM-03 Quarterly access review"The IT Security Manager runs a review of all user and privileged accounts on in-scope systems every quarter. System owners confirm each account is still needed and its access is correct. Access not confirmed within 10 working days is removed. This prevents excess or leftover access. Proof: signed review records and removal tickets."
        TypeDetective + corrective · manual with tool support · quarterly · key control
        ISO 27002 attributesConfidentiality, integrity · Protect · Identity & access management · Protection
        TestPick 2 quarters and 5 systems. Check each review happened on time, covered all accounts, and every unconfirmed account was removed within 10 working days.
  7Link everything up (traceability)
    What you do: check the mapping table answers questions in both directions.
      QuestionAnswer from the mapping table
      Which controls deliver CSF PR.AA?IAM-01 to IAM-06
      Which controls implement ISO A.5.18 (for the SoA)?IAM-01, IAM-02, IAM-03
      If we retire IAM-03, what loses coverage?CSF PR.AA-05-d, ISO A.5.18-b and CIS 5.1-b all drop to uncovered. So it stays.
      Are we at CIS IG2?Filter to the 130 IG2 safeguards and check each one has at least one control with full coverage and a passing test

  10Keep it alive → governance
    What you do: name an owner, set the rhythm, and list what forces an update.
        OwnerHead of GRC owns the library. Each domain has a named control owner.
        RhythmOwners confirm their controls every year. GRC checks for ISO, NIST and CIS updates every quarter. The mapping table is versioned (v1.0 at handover).
        TriggersA new framework version · an ISO audit finding · a new major system · an incident · a change in risk appetite
        ExampleWhen CIS moved to v8.1 and added Govern, the owner re-tagged the affected safeguards, re-ran coverage, and found no control changes were needed. A one-hour update instead of a re-mapping project.
  How well do two things match?
  These terms come from NIST's mapping guidance (NIST IR 8477). Always read them as "requirement → control".
    Match typePlain meaningCoverageExample
    EqualExactly the same thingFullRare. Be suspicious if you see lots of these.
    Requirement is smallerThe control covers all of it, and moreFullCIS 1.1 (device inventory) sits inside ISO A.5.9 (inventory of all assets)
    Requirement is biggerThe control only covers part of itPartialNIS2 asks for MFA and secure communications; the control only does MFA
    They overlapEach has something the other doesn'tPartialDORA incident reporting vs a general incident control. DORA adds its own deadlines.
    No matchNothing in commonNoneLogged as a gap, so a new control is needed
  Bonus point: the Secure Controls Framework (SCF) uses the same idea and adds a 1–10 strength score.
  The control-writing formula
    1 · Whothe owner or role
    2 · Does whata clear action
    3 · Whenhow often or what triggers it
    4 · Howthe method or standard
    5 · Whythe risk it prevents
    6 · Proofthe evidence it leaves
  Worked example: MFA
    ✗ Weak"Multi-factor authentication should be implemented where appropriate."
No owner, no scope, no timing, nothing to test.
    ✓ Strong"The IAM Lead makes sure phishing-resistant MFA is enforced through the company's login system for all remote access, all admin access and all internet-facing systems with sensitive data, every time someone logs in. The CISO approves any exception, and exceptions are reviewed every quarter. This prevents break-ins using stolen passwords. Proof: the login-policy export and the quarterly exception review."
      GoalOnly the right people get into systems and data
      TypePreventive · automated · always on (exceptions checked quarterly)
      Maps toISO A.8.5 · CSF PR.AA-03 · CIS 6.3, 6.4, 6.5 · Essential Eight MFA · NIS2 Art 21(2)(j) · DORA Art 9(4)(d)
      How you test itCheck the policy covers all three groups. Sample admin accounts and remote logins: zero without MFA. Every exception has an approval and a quarterly review.
  Goal vs control vs test
    Control objectiveThe result you want.
"Only authorised people access systems."
    ControlWhat someone does to get it.
"The IAM Lead enforces MFA…"
    Assessment indicatorHow you prove it works.
"Sample 25 admin accounts: none without MFA."
  What to record for each control
  Good line to use: "I'd start with the five ISO 27002 attribute types, because they're a recognised standard, then add what this client needs."
      ISO 27002 attributes
        Control type: preventive, detective, corrective
        Protects: confidentiality, integrity, availability
        Cyber concept: identify, protect, detect, respond, recover (add "govern" from CSF 2.0)
        Capability: 15 areas, such as asset management, access, continuity, suppliers
        Security domain: governance, protection, defence, resilience
      Add for real-world use
        ID, title, goal, the control text
        Owner and who runs it day to day
        Manual or automated, and how often
        Where it applies: entity, country, system type
        Key settings (e.g. "patch within 48 hours") and their source
        Linked rules and risks
        How to test it and what evidence to collect
        Last review date and change history
  Cleaning up a messy library
    If you see…Then…
    Two controls doing the same jobMerge them; keep both sets of links
    One control doing several jobsSplit it into single-purpose controls
    A control linked to no rule and no riskRemove it, or justify why it stays
    Something that reads like a principleMove it up into policy
    Step-by-step instructionsMove it down into a procedure; keep the outcome as the control
    Sources with different numbersUse the strictest, or make a country-specific version if they truly conflict

  Keeping it alive after handover
    WhoOne library owner (usually the CISO office). Control owners confirm their controls each year. The risk team challenges and internal audit tests.
    WhenA full review every year, a quick regulatory check every quarter, plus an update whenever a law, audit or incident triggers one.
    HowVersion numbers and a change log. Someone watches for new regulations. Traceability shows what a change affects.
    Health checks% of rules fully covered · controls with no owner or test · controls overdue for review.
  Mistakes to avoid
      Mapping frameworks to each other instead of to one master list. It gets unmanageable fast.
      Matching on keywords. Two things both called "logging" can mean different things.
      Calling partial matches full. That hides the real gaps.
      Controls that do several things. You can't test them cleanly.
      Copying one framework as the library. ISO's 93 controls won't hold DORA's deadlines or APRA's specifics.
      Using the weakest number. Dropping E8's 48-hour patch time or DORA's 4-hour report.
      Forgetting where rules apply in a group with many entities and countries.
      No owner after handover. The library goes stale within a year.

Released Feb 2024. Six functions, 22 categories, 106 subcategories. Now meant for every organisation, not just critical infrastructure.

      DE.CM Continuous monitoringDE.AE Adverse event analysis

      RC.RP Recovery plan executionRC.CO Recovery comms
  Changed from 1.1: Govern added, supply chain moved into Govern, "Improvement" added, Protect reorganised, plus online examples, references, quick-start guides and community profiles.
      Tiers are not maturity levels
        Partial: ad hoc
        Risk Informed: approved, but not organisation-wide
        Repeatable: formal policy, kept up to date
        Adaptive: learns and adapts; part of the culture
      Say this clearlyTiers describe how rigorous the organisation's overall approach is. NIST says CSF is not a maturity model. For maturity scores, define your own scale (e.g. 0–5) and say it's your method.
      Profiles
        Current profile: where you are now
        Target profile: where you want to be
        Community profile: a shared target for a sector
        Gap = target − current, which becomes your action plan
        The control library is the "how" behind each target outcome
  Scoring several entities at once
    Same scale everywhereOne scoring scale and one standard of evidence for every entity. Calibrate assessors on a pilot first.
    Score shared controls onceGroup-wide controls (like a central login system or SOC) are scored once and inherited. Only local controls are scored per entity.
    Roll up step by stepSubcategory → category → function, using a method you state up front.
    Never show just the averageWeight by importance, but always show the weakest entity too. Averages hide the weak spot, and the weak spot is where the breach happens.
    Different targets are fineA small subsidiary doesn't need the regulated parent's target. Measure each against its own.
    Show it visuallyA heatmap of entity × category, plus gap-to-target bars.
  Building the roadmap
      Group gaps into projects, not one task per gap
      Prioritise by gap size × importance × regulatory pressure
      Basics first: governance, asset list, access control. Then detection and response.
      Give each wave an owner, effort, cost range and a measure of success
      Reassess on the same scale so progress is comparable year to year

      Controls vs Benchmarks
      Controls = what to do. A prioritised list that works on any technology.
      Benchmarks = how to configure one specific product (Windows, AWS, a database). Level 1 and Level 2 settings, checked with CIS-CAT.
      Benchmarks are the detail behind Control 4 (secure configuration).
      How CIS fits the library
        Great source of tests, because safeguards are concrete
        IGs work as a baseline level (e.g. "all entities meet IG1")
        Light on governance and suppliers, so pair it with CSF or ISO

Other frameworks
You need to know what each one is and how it fits, not every clause. If pushed, say honestly you haven't used it hands-on, then show you know the structure.
    NCSC CAF v4.0 · UK
      What it isAn outcome-based assessment framework from the UK's NCSC. Version 4.0 came out on 6 Aug 2025.
      Who it coversUK critical services and their regulators, and UK government (via GovAssure)
      How it's built4 objectives → 14 principles → 41 outcomes → "indicators of good practice". Each outcome is rated Achieved, Partially achieved or Not achieved.
      New in v4.0Understanding threats (A2.b), secure software development (A4.b), understanding user and system behaviour (C1.f), threat hunting (C2.b), and AI risks. Up from 39 outcomes.
      Fits the library asThe indicators are basically ready-made tests. Map each outcome to several controls.
    Essential Eight · Australia
      What it isEight priority defences from ASD, plus a maturity model (latest version: Nov 2023).
      Who it coversRequired at Level 2 for Australian federal government agencies, and widely expected of regulated companies
      The eightPatch apps · Patch OS · MFA · Restrict admin rights · Application control · Restrict Office macros · App hardening · Regular backups
      LevelsML0 to ML3, based on how skilled the attacker is. You must reach a level across all eight before moving up.
      NumbersCritical internet-facing patches within 48 hours. The 2023 update tightened MFA rules.
      Fits the library asSettings and level tags on existing controls. It's not a full library: no governance, incident or supplier coverage.
    APRA CPS 234 · Information security (Australia)
      What it isA binding rule from APRA, in force since July 2019. Guidance is in CPG 234.
      Who it coversAll APRA-regulated entities: banks, insurers, super funds
      Key asksThe board is accountable · classify information assets by how critical and sensitive they are · controls to match · tested incident plans · regular independent control testing · check third parties
      NumbersTell APRA within 72 hours of a material incident, and within 10 business days of a serious control weakness you can't fix quickly

  Mapping and the library
  Q1Walk me through how you'd build a unified control library. Most likely
    "The core idea is simple: one master list, every source mapped to it once, and every control linked to a test. I'd do it in four phases."
      Prepare: list every source and where it applies, break each into single requirements, and agree the master structure (domains, ID format, attributes).
      Map: map each requirement to the controls with a match type and a reason, clean up duplicates, and write each control properly.
      Prove: check traceability both ways, then compare against today's controls to build the gap register.
      Plan and sustain: group gaps into a roadmap, and set up the governance that keeps it current.
    Example you can use"I've done this with ISO 27001, NIST CSF and DPDPA. I used Annex A as the base, broke all three into single requirements, and mapped them. ISO and CSF overlapped heavily, while DPDPA was mostly partial, so I added privacy controls. In the end, one test counted for all three."
    "What would you do in week one?" Build the source register, get the master structure agreed, and pilot one domain end to end, for example Identity & Access: break it down, map, write, test. That proves the method before scaling it to every domain.
  Q2How do you choose the master framework (the anchor)?
    "I use three tests: is it detailed enough to test, broad enough to cover every source, and familiar to the people who'll report on it?"
      OptionStrengthWeakness
      ISO 27001 Annex A / 27002Familiar, ties to certification, has attributesFairly high-level (93 controls)
      NIST SP 800-53Very detailed, has adjustable settingsHeavy: over a thousand controls with enhancements
      NIST CSF 2.0Great for board reportingOutcomes, not controls
      CIS v8.1Very testableLight on governance and suppliers

    Example you can use"In my unified framework I used Annex A as the base because the client was already certified, so it meant the least change. I kept CSF as a reporting tag, not the base, because CSF lists outcomes, not controls."
    "Would you use Annex A here too?" Probably not on its own. This client has APRA, DORA, NIS2 and MAS, with deadlines, registers and tolerance limits that Annex A can't hold without heavy extension. I'd use the client's own domains with ISO 27002 / 800-53 level of detail and CSF for reporting, and decide it in the first workshop.
  Q3What makes a good control statement? Give an example.
    "A good control says who does what, when, how, why, and what evidence it leaves, and it does only one thing."
      Words I remove: "should", "where appropriate", "periodically", "as needed". Each one makes the control untestable.
      Step-by-step detail goes into the procedure. The control keeps the outcome and the frequency.
      My check: could an auditor write the test from the statement alone?
      ✗ Weak"User access is reviewed periodically."
      ✓ Strong"The IT Security Manager runs a review of all user and privileged accounts on in-scope systems every quarter. System owners confirm each account is still needed. Unconfirmed access is removed within 10 working days. This prevents excess access. Evidence: signed review records and removal tickets."
    "Who decides the 10 days?" The client, based on risk appetite. I'd propose a value, cite any source that sets one, and record the decision.
  Q4Two sources set different requirements for the same thing. What do you do?
    "I keep one control and make the number a setting. The strictest value that applies wins, and I record where it came from."
      That's the same idea as NIST 800-53's "organisation-defined parameters".
      If the rules apply in different countries, I don't force the strictest on everyone. The setting varies by jurisdiction.
      If the strictest isn't achievable, it becomes a gap with a plan, or a formally accepted risk. Never a quiet downgrade.

  Q5The client has 800 controls. How do you slim it down?
    "Map first, then cut. Duplicates only become visible once everything sits on the same structure."
      Map every existing control to the master structure. Where several land on the same objective, you've found duplicates.
      Merge them, split controls that do several jobs, push procedural detail down into procedures, and remove controls with no rule or risk behind them.
      Before removing anything, check traceability: no requirement can lose coverage.
      Give owners a before-and-after list with a reason for every change.
    Example you can use"A common pattern is five password controls written after five different audits. They become one authentication control plus a password standard. Same coverage, and owners evidence it once instead of five times."
    "How do you get owners to agree to losing 'their' control?" Show them two things: nothing loses coverage, and their evidence work goes down. That usually ends the argument.
  Q6Control objective vs control vs assessment indicator?
    "The objective is the result you want, the control is what someone does to get it, and the indicator is how you prove it works."
    Example you can use: patching
        ObjectiveVulnerabilities are fixed before they can be exploited
        ControlIT Operations patches critical vulnerabilities on internet-facing systems within the agreed deadline (e.g. 14 days), tracked in the vulnerability tool
        IndicatorDesign: the deadlines are defined and approved. Operating: sample 25 critical vulnerabilities and check each was fixed in time. Metric: % fixed within deadline (target ≥ 95%)
    "KPI, KCI or KRI?" A KPI measures how a process performs, a KCI measures whether a control works, and a KRI tracks how risk exposure is trending. "% patched on time" is a KCI. "Number of exploitable internet-facing vulnerabilities" is a KRI.
  Q7What would you record for each control?
    "I start with the five ISO 27002 attribute types, because they're a recognised standard, then add what the client needs to run and test it."
      ISO 27002: control type, what it protects (CIA), cyber concept, capability, security domain. Add "govern" from CSF 2.0.
      Practical: owner, manual or automated, frequency, where it applies, settings and their source, linked requirements and risks, how to test it, evidence, review dates.
    Example you can use: IAM-03 access reviewDetective · manual with tool support · quarterly · key control · owner: IT Security Manager · applies to: all entities, all in-scope systems · maps to ISO A.5.18, CSF PR.AA-05, CIS 5.1 · evidence: signed review records.
    "How do you stop it turning into 40 columns?" Every field needs a user: a report, a filter or a test. If nobody will use it, it doesn't go in.
  Q8How do you keep the library up to date after we leave?
    "Three things: a named owner, a regular rhythm, and clear triggers for an update."
      The library is owned by the second line (CISO office or GRC). Individual controls are owned by the first line.
      Rhythm: a full yearly review, a quarterly regulatory check, and an annual sign-off by owners.
      Triggers: a new law or framework version, an audit finding, an incident, a new major system.
      Health checks: % of requirements fully covered, controls with no owner or test, and overdue reviews.
    Example you can use"Take MAS's 2026 consultation on its technology-risk Notices. With a governed library, horizon scanning flags it, we map the changed requirements, traceability shows which controls are affected (change management and backup), we update the settings, and owners re-confirm. That's days of work, not a new project."
  ISO 27001
  Q9What changed in ISO 27001:2022?
    "The big change was Annex A: 114 controls in 14 domains became 93 in 4 themes, with 11 new ones."
      The four themes: Organizational 37, People 8, Physical 14, Technological 34.
      The 11 new controls include threat intelligence, cloud security, configuration management, data leakage prevention and secure coding.
      In the main clauses: new 6.3 planning of changes, more focus on processes in 4.4 and 8.1, and audit and management review split into sub-clauses.
      The 2024 amendment added climate change to clauses 4.1 and 4.2. The deadline to move off the 2013 version was 31 Oct 2025.
      ISO 27002:2022 added attributes to each control, which are useful for tagging a library.
    Example you can use"In a typical transition, most of the work is re-mapping the old controls to the new numbering and updating the SoA. The real gaps are usually in the new controls, especially threat intelligence and configuration management, because those need actual processes, not just a renamed document."
  Q10What goes in a Statement of Applicability? Can you leave Annex A controls out?
    "The SoA lists the controls you need, why, whether they're in place, and why any Annex A control is excluded."
      Yes, you can exclude Annex A controls, with a reason, usually because the risk doesn't exist.
      Annex A is a checklist to compare against. The controls themselves come from risk treatment.
      So the SoA can include controls from outside Annex A.
    Example you can use"In my unified framework, the DPDPA privacy controls, like consent and breach notification to the Data Protection Board, sat in the same SoA as the Annex A controls. One document, one audit trail."
    "Example of a valid exclusion?" Excluding 8.28 secure coding when there's no in-house development. But if development is outsourced, 8.30 (outsourced development) still applies, which is a common trap.

  Q13How did you combine maturity scores across several entities?
    "Same scale everywhere, shared controls scored once, and I never show the average without the weakest entity."
      Calibrate assessors on one entity first, with clear evidence required for each level.
      Group-wide services (central identity, SOC) are scored once and inherited. Only local controls are scored per entity.
      Roll up subcategory → category → function using a method stated up front.
      Each entity is measured against its own target.
    Example you can use"Say three entities score 3.2, 3.4 and 1.8 on Detect. The average is 2.8, which looks acceptable. But the entity at 1.8 is where an attack goes unnoticed. So the report shows all three against their targets, and the weak one gets its own roadmap item."

      Then "are we at Level 2?" is a filter on test results, not a separate assessment.

    "I'll be straight: I know their structure and key requirements, but I haven't mapped them hands-on. The method doesn't change, though."
      Read the regulator's own text and guidance first, not summaries.
      Break it into single requirements and map them to the master list.
      Flag what's genuinely new, then check my mapping with someone on the team who's done it before.
    Example you can say"If I got CPS 230 tomorrow, I'd read the standard and its guidance, break it into requirements, and flag the three things most libraries don't have yet: critical operations, tolerance levels, and the material service provider register. The rest maps onto existing resilience and supplier controls."
  Q24What's a weakness that's relevant to this role?
    "I can over-build. My instinct is to capture every attribute and edge case."
      My fix: every field has to have a user (a report, a filter or a test) before it goes in.
      I also timebox the detail per domain and check with the manager before going deeper.
      With a mid-December deadline, on-time and usable beats perfect.
